### Linking them together
#### The Brandenburg connection

The cues in the medley segue seamlessly except, for Unsolved Mysteries-to-Darkplace. 
For it, I composed linking material. 

And trust me, it is the greatest link since Bach took 30 seconds to pen the second movement of Brandenburg Concerto No. 3.  

If you are a fan of brevity, I have you covered. While the Adagio from Brandenburg 3 contains two entire chords \* [^1] , my segue contains exactly one note! One note is all it takes to walk us up the key change between the two themes. 

If you are particularly invested in my effort, you’ll be glad to know that the custom synthesizer patch used for the one-note segue employs two low pass filters and four oscillators, so forethought was exercised. It’s not like I just turned on a preset machine and punched a key at random.  


### Recording and assembly 

I approached this somewhat in reverse order, reverse engineering Darkplace first. Actually once I decided that the composer probably used something like a Yamaha XG guitar (like in the X-Men cartoon theme) to evoke period flavor, the entire arrangement flowed from there. The arrangement was also informed by the T.J. Hooker theme (as mentioned in the video).

After I’d completed Darkplace, I was able to complete Tales from the Darkside on a Sunday afternoon,  I’d previously made a bell sound on a Korg Poly 800 Mk II that sounded like the Roland Juno 60. "Tales from the Darkside" came together quickly because it *wanted* to be played on 80s synthesizers!

### Footnote 
#### Bach, Wendy Carlos, and being creative with a Brandenburg

[^1]:  \* I was always taught that Bach intended the second movement to be guidance for players to improvise something suitable. It’s always amusing to me when I hear a major recording of the 3rd Branderburg and the second movement just consists of “Dah…DUM!”.

Among the best Adagios ever recorded was Wendy Carlos’ thoughtful and arresting exercise in “Switched on Bach” (labeled "Brandenburg Concerto No. 3 in G Major - Second Movement" on the recording).

Carlos approaches the Second Movement as a fantasia as opposed to slavishly imitating a Bach Adagio. Most commentary will focus on the unnatural electronic sounds employed, but ignoring the non-imitative Moog tambres, I get impressions of both nature and the inner workings of the human mind. If the Brandenburgs were Bach’s attempt to expand the tonal pallet in imperial splendor, Carlos allows Bach to step outside the courtly interiors into the gardens with wind, thunder and memory. Carlos Adagio is the sound of thinking things over.
